2. CLINICAL AND EPIDEMIOLOGICAL BACKGROUND
================================================================================

2.1 Burden and the detection gap

  WHO estimates that 10.7 million people fell ill with TB in 2024 (95%
  uncertainty interval 9.9 to 11.5 million) and that 1.23 million died (95% UI
  1.13 to 1.33 million). The incidence rate was 131 per 100 000 population per
  year and the case fatality rate 11.5%. About 8.3 million people were newly
  diagnosed and officially notified, which is 78% (95% UI 72 to 84%) of the
  estimated incident cases. The remaining gap is roughly 2.4 million people who
  were never reached by a diagnosis.  [WHO-GTB-2025]

  The implication for this project: the bottleneck addressed by a screening
  tool is case finding, not treatment. TB is largely curable; the people who
  die are disproportionately the people never diagnosed.

2.2 Chest radiography in screening, and the CAD recommendation

  WHO includes chest radiography among the tools for systematic TB screening
  and triage. Since 2021 WHO has recommended that computer-aided detection
  (CAD) software may be used in place of a human reader for interpreting
  digital chest radiographs, for screening and triage of TB disease, among
  people aged 15 years and older, in populations in which TB screening is
  recommended.  [WHO-SCREEN-2021]

  Note the scope of that recommendation carefully when citing it:
    - It applies to people aged 15 and over. Not children.
    - It applies in populations where screening is already recommended.
    - It concerns screening and triage. Not diagnosis.
    - The evidence base was diagnostic accuracy against bacteriological
      confirmation as the reference standard.

2.3 Radiographic appearance of pulmonary TB

  TB does not present one way. Upper-zone infiltrates, cavitation, nodular
  shadowing, pleural effusion and miliary patterns all occur. Some people with
  bacteriologically confirmed TB have a chest radiograph read as normal, and
  this is more common among people living with HIV.  [WHO-CXR-2016]

2.4 Specificity is the problem, not sensitivity

  The same radiographic findings are produced by pneumonia, lung cancer,
  fungal infection, healed scarring from previous TB, and other chronic lung
  disease. Chest radiography is comparatively sensitive and comparatively
  non-specific for TB. It narrows a differential; it does not resolve one.
  [WHO-CXR-2016]

2.5 Confirmation is bacteriological

  A TB diagnosis rests on tests for the organism: WHO-recommended rapid
  molecular tests, culture, or smear microscopy, interpreted alongside the
  clinical picture. An image-based model sits upstream of all of that. Its
  output is a reason to test, never a substitute for testing.
  [WHO-SCREEN-2021]

2.6 The distinction the whole project rests on

  Screening sorts a population into "test further" and "probably not now".
  Diagnosis concludes what is wrong with one person and carries a treatment
  decision and accountability. A model that reads an image can participate in
  the first. It cannot perform the second.

3. RESEARCH QUESTIONS
================================================================================

All three are open. None has been tested in this project. Each is stated with
a hypothesis and with evaluation criteria fixed in advance, so that it is later
possible to notice that the evidence was never gathered.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
RQ1 - DETECTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question
  Within a single selected dataset, can an image classifier distinguish
  radiographs labelled TB-related from those labelled non-TB, at a level that
  would be useful for triage rather than merely better than chance?

Hypothesis
  A convolutional backbone pretrained on natural images and fine-tuned on
  frontal chest radiographs reaches a sensitivity and specificity worth
  measuring on a held-out split of the same dataset.

Evaluation criteria
  1. Sensitivity and specificity reported together at a stated decision
     threshold. Never accuracy alone.
  2. Patient-level train / validation / test splits, so no person appears on
     both sides of a split.
  3. A test split never used for model selection or threshold tuning.
  4. Confidence intervals on every reported rate, given how few TB-positive
     cases most public datasets contain.

Current state
  Open. No model has been trained or evaluated, so there is no result either
  way.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
RQ2 - GENERALIZATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question
  How does performance change when the model is applied to images from a
  different source, one with different equipment, exposure habits, patient
  population and disease prevalence, than the one it was trained on?

Hypothesis
  Performance drops on an external source, and the drop is larger than the
  usual split-to-split variation. Whether the drop is small enough to tolerate
  is the actual question.

Evaluation criteria
  1. At least one external dataset evaluated with the threshold fixed in
     advance.
  2. In-domain and external results reported side by side, not separately.
  3. Calibration assessed on the external set, not only discrimination.
  4. Positive predictive value recomputed at the prevalence of the intended
     deployment setting.

Current state
  Open and untouched. External validation is the part most easily skipped and
  the part that decides whether a model means anything outside its own
  dataset.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
RQ3 - INTERPRETABILITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question
  Can gradient-based saliency methods help a researcher inspect which image
  regions drove a prediction, and can that inspection catch a model that is
  right for the wrong reason?

Hypothesis
  Saliency maps are useful for finding gross failures, such as a model keying
  on a laterality marker, a text burn-in or the image border, but they do not
  establish that the model uses clinically meaningful features.

Evaluation criteria
  1. Saliency computed from the deployed weights, not drawn by hand.
  2. Maps reviewed against lesion annotations where those exist.
  3. Sanity checks applied, for example randomised-weight comparisons.
  4. Agreement, or disagreement, with a radiologist's reading recorded for a
     sample of cases.

Current state
  Open. No saliency map has been computed.

4.3 The reference standard decides what study you are running

  If the TB label was assigned by reading the radiograph, a model trained on
  it learns to imitate a radiologist, and its ceiling is that radiologist's
  agreement rate. If the label came from bacteriological confirmation, the
  model is being asked a harder and more useful question. These two studies
  look identical in a metrics table and are not the same study. State which
  one you ran.

4.4 Patient-level splitting is not a detail

  Two radiographs of the same person, one in training and one in test, let a
  model recognise the person rather than the disease. The result is a high
  score that collapses on new patients. It is among the most frequently
  repeated errors in chest-imaging machine learning, and it is undetectable
  from the metrics alone, which is why the split strategy belongs in the
  dataset table next to the sample size.  [PITFALLS-2021]

5. METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

The order is not cosmetic. Splitting before preprocessing, and validation
before testing, are what keep the final number meaningful.

  Stage 1  DATASET ACQUISITION
    Obtain a dataset with a documented reference standard and licence terms
    that permit research use. Record the version and the download date.
    Output: dataset snapshot, licence record, citation.

  Stage 2  DATA VERIFICATION
    Check for duplicates, corrupt files, lateral or paediatric images that do
    not belong, burned-in text, and laterality markers a model could exploit.
    Output: exclusion log with a count and a reason per excluded image.

  Stage 3  PATIENT-LEVEL SPLITTING
    Split by person, never by image. See 4.4.
    Output: train / validation / test manifests with class counts per split.

  Stage 4  IMAGE PREPROCESSING
    Fix a resize, an intensity normalisation and an augmentation policy, then
    apply them identically everywhere. Augmentation touches the training split
    only.
    Output: a versioned preprocessing specification.

  Stage 5  MODEL TRAINING
    Train from a pretrained backbone with an explicit class-imbalance
    strategy, a fixed seed and a logged configuration. Record the full
    hyperparameter set, not a summary of it.
    Output: checkpoint, training log, configuration file.

  Stage 6  VALIDATION
    Use the validation split for model selection and for choosing the
    operating threshold. Both decisions consume this split, which is why it
    cannot also serve as the test set.
    Output: selected checkpoint, chosen threshold, and the sweep behind it.

  Stage 7  INDEPENDENT TESTING
    Score the frozen model, at the frozen threshold, exactly once, on data
    untouched until that moment. Preferably from a different source than the
    training data.
    Output: test metrics with confidence intervals.

  Stage 8  PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS
    Report discrimination, calibration, the confusion matrix, subgroup
    behaviour where labels permit, and a review of the cases the model got
    wrong.
    Output: evaluation report and error analysis.

5.1 Validation and testing are not interchangeable

  The validation split picks the checkpoint and sets the threshold. Both of
  those decisions consume it. Score the model on that same split and the
  result is optimistic by an unknown amount. An independent test set is scored
  once, with everything frozen, and once it has been used to make a decision
  it is a validation set.

  threshold as something to calibrate against the population being screened,
  rather than a constant shipped with the software. A threshold tuned on one
  cohort does not transfer to another with different prevalence and a
  different cost of a missed case.  [WHO-CAD-TOOLKIT]

6. EVALUATION FRAMEWORK
================================================================================

Each metric answers a different question. Reporting one and omitting the rest
is how a weak model is made to look strong.

  SENSITIVITY (RECALL)              TP / (TP + FN)
    Of the people the reference standard calls TB-positive, the share the
    model flags. A screening tool is judged here first: a missed case leaves
    someone untreated and infectious.
    Caveat: rises as the threshold is lowered, at the cost of specificity.
    Quoting it without the threshold is meaningless.

  SPECIFICITY                       TN / (TN + FP)
    Of the people the reference standard calls TB-negative, the share the
    model leaves unflagged. It governs how much unnecessary confirmatory
    testing the tool creates.
    Caveat: in a low-prevalence population even 95% specificity can produce
    more false positives than true positives.

  PRECISION (POSITIVE PREDICTIVE VALUE)     TP / (TP + FP)
    Of the people the model flags, the share the reference standard confirms.
    This is the number a clinician implicitly asks about: if it flags, how
    often is it right?
    Caveat: depends on prevalence. The same model has a very different PPV in
    a TB clinic and in a general screening queue.

  F1-SCORE          2 * (precision * recall) / (precision + recall)
    Harmonic mean of precision and recall, as a single summary for ranking
    configurations.
    Caveat: hides the trade-off it averages, and ignores true negatives
    entirely. Useful for model selection, not for clinical reasoning.

  ROC-AUC                           integral of TPR d(FPR)
    How well the score separates the two labelled classes across every
    threshold at once. 0.5 is chance; 1.0 is perfect separation on that data.
    Caveat: threshold-free, so it says nothing about behaviour at the
    threshold you would actually deploy, and nothing about calibration.

  CONFUSION MATRIX                  [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]]
    The four raw counts at one threshold. Every rate above is a ratio of these
    numbers, which is why the counts should be published alongside the rates.
    Caveat: only interpretable with the class support stated. Four cells
    computed over 40 cases are not evidence.

  CALIBRATION                       P(y = 1 | score = s) approx. s
    Whether a score of 0.8 corresponds to an 80% observed positive rate.
    Required before any score is described as a probability.
    Caveat: a model can discriminate well and be badly calibrated.
    Uncalibrated scores must not be presented as risk.

6.1 Accuracy is not usefulness

  On the mock test split described in section 7, a model that flags nothing at
  all scores 82.5% accuracy, because 594 of the 720 images are non-TB. It
  would also miss every case of tuberculosis. High accuracy on an imbalanced
  dataset is not evidence of clinical value, and on its own it is barely
  evidence of anything.

7.1 What this section is, and is not

  None of this was measured. It is a hand-built example of what a complete
  evaluation report looks like, built so the reporting layout could be
  reviewed. Do not carry any figure from this section into the paper as a
  result.

  Two rules were followed while writing these numbers, because a mock dataset
  that contradicts itself teaches the wrong things:

    1. Internal consistency. The confusion matrix, the rates, the threshold
       sweep and the curves all describe the same imagined 720-image test
       split. Rates are the actual ratios of the counts, the operating points
       sit on the plotted ROC curve, and the quoted AUC is the trapezoidal
       area under the points that are drawn.
    2. Realistic shape. A positive predictive value of 0.38 at 17.5% positive
       prevalence, falling further at screening prevalence, is what a credible
       chest X-ray screening model looks like. Numbers in the high nineties
       would be a nicer demo and a worse education.

7.11 Analyses deliberately absent

  Each of these would change how the numbers above should be read.

  Calibration curve
    Needs per-image predicted scores paired with reference labels, binned and
    plotted against the observed positive rate.

  Subgroup performance
    Needs age, sex, HIV status and comorbidity labels. No public chest X-ray
    set used in TB research distributes them.

  External validation
    Needs images from a source that took no part in development, scored at the
    frozen threshold.

  Reader comparison
    Needs independent readings of the same images by qualified radiologists,
    for an agreement analysis.

  Confidence intervals
    Needs bootstrap resampling over the test split. Required for every rate,
    and especially so with only 126 positive cases.

8. ERROR ANALYSIS
================================================================================

Each failure mode is paired with its cost to a person, not only its effect on
a metric. Three of them produce metrics that look fine while the model is
broken.

  FALSE NEGATIVES
    Cost: a person with TB is told nothing was found.
    Early or minimal disease, miliary patterns, and TB in people living with
    HIV are all under-represented in public datasets and all harder to see. A
    model that never sees them in training will not flag them in deployment.

  FALSE POSITIVES
    Cost: confirmatory testing, travel and anxiety for someone without TB.
    Pneumonia, healed scarring, malignancy and chronic lung disease produce
    overlapping appearances. At realistic screening prevalence, false
    positives will outnumber true positives for most plausible operating
    points. See 7.8.

  IMAGE QUALITY
    Cost: an unreliable output presented with the same confidence as a
    reliable one.
    Underexposure, rotation, poor inspiration, portable films and photographs
    of printed films all shift the input distribution. Without a quality gate,
    the model scores them anyway.

  ACQUISITION SHIFT
    Cost: performance that silently decays after deployment.
    Detector hardware, post-processing pipelines and local protocols differ
    between facilities. A model can learn a site signature instead of a
    disease signature and look excellent until it moves.

  DATASET LEAKAGE
    Cost: metrics that are confidently wrong.
    Duplicate images, or two radiographs of one patient split across train and
    test, produce high scores that do not transfer. Without patient
    identifiers this cannot be ruled out, only reported as a risk.
    [PITFALLS-2021]

  CLASS IMBALANCE
    Cost: a majority-class model that still reports high accuracy.
    Public TB sets are heavily skewed toward non-TB images. Accuracy is the
    wrong summary; per-class recall and the raw counts are the right ones.

  POPULATION SHIFT
    Cost: a threshold that is wrong for the people it is used on.
    Age structure, HIV prevalence, prior TB history, and whether screening is
    symptom-triggered or community-wide all change the right operating point.

8.1 Reading the two error cells

  In the mock matrix at 7.4, 13 missed and 184 false alarms are not
  interchangeable. The 13 are people who leave with a negative screen and an
  untreated infection. The 184 are people sent for a test they did not need, a
  real cost in travel, time and anxiety, but a recoverable one. Where the
  threshold sits is a judgement about which cost the programme can carry, and
  it is not a judgement a model can make.

8.2 Failure case studies

  Reserved and deliberately empty. Filling this section requires misclassified
  images from a real evaluation run, reviewed with a clinician, published with
  consent and de-identification. A plausible-sounding patient case written for
  a slide is fabricated clinical material.

9. EXPLAINABILITY
================================================================================

9.1 What Grad-CAM does

  Grad-CAM and related methods take the gradient of a class score with respect
  to a late convolutional feature map and project it back onto the image,
  producing a coarse heatmap of regions the score was sensitive to. For a
  convolutional classifier like the one specified in 5.2, it is available
  essentially for free.  [GRADCAM-2017]

9.2 What it is genuinely useful for

  Catching gross failures. A model that keys on a laterality marker, a
  burned-in timestamp, the image border, or the characteristic post-processing
  of one hospital's equipment will usually show it in a saliency map, and that
  discovery is worth more than the metric it invalidates.

9.3 What it does not establish

  It does not show that the highlighted region is a lesion, or that the model
  used clinically meaningful features. Saliency maps have been shown to look
  convincing when produced from randomised weights, which means a
  plausible-looking heatmap is not evidence of anything on its own.
  [SANITY-2018]

  A saliency map is not a segmentation, not a measurement, and not a
  diagnosis. It does not say the model found a cavity in the right upper zone;
  it says the score was sensitive to that part of the image. Those are
  different statements, and an overlay presented to a clinician without that
  distinction attached invites the stronger reading every time.

9.4 Review protocol for a real explainability section

  1. Original radiograph, unmodified.
  2. Model explanation overlay, computed from the deployed weights on that
     exact image. Never drawn by hand.
  3. Research interpretation, recording:
       - region overlap with lesion annotations, where they exist
       - independent radiologist agreement on a sampled subset
       - sanity check against randomised model weights
       - behaviour on images with markers or text burned in


================================================================================
10. GENERALIZATION, LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
================================================================================

10.1 Topics that stand between a benchmark number and a useful tool

  External validation
    An evaluation on data from a source that played no part in development.
    Until that exists, reported performance describes one dataset, not a
    capability.  [PITFALLS-2021]

  Dataset shift
    Changes in equipment, exposure habits, patient mix or disease prevalence
    between development and deployment. All of them move the operating point,
    and prevalence moves positive predictive value most.

  Demographic representation
    Performance should be reported per subgroup, by age, sex, HIV status and
    comorbidity, wherever labels permit. Most public chest X-ray sets ship no
    demographic fields at all, which makes the analysis impossible rather than
    unnecessary.

  Calibration
    Discrimination and calibration are separate properties. A score may rank
    cases well and still be a poor estimate of risk, and only a calibrated
    score can be described as a probability.  [WHO-CAD-TOOLKIT]

  Threshold recalibration
    WHO guidance treats the operating threshold as something to set locally
    against the population being screened, not a constant shipped with the
    software.  [WHO-CAD-TOOLKIT]

  Prospective evaluation
    Retrospective accuracy on archived images and prospective usefulness in a
    live screening programme are different claims. The second requires
    measuring what happened to the people screened.

  Workflow integration
    Who reads the output, how a flag is resolved, what happens when
    confirmatory testing is unavailable, and who is accountable for a missed
    case. A model that improves a metric can still make a service worse.

  Privacy and data governance
    Chest radiographs are health data. Consent, retention, de-identification,
    cross-border transfer and the right to withdraw all need answers before
    any image leaves a device. The prototype avoids the question by never
    transmitting one.

10.2 Retrospective evaluation is not clinical deployment

  These two activities are routinely reported as though a good result in the
  left column implied readiness for the right. It does not. The left column
  licenses one thing: a reason to attempt the right.

  Axis                        Retrospective research      Clinical deployment
  --------------------------  --------------------------  --------------------
  Question asked              Can the model separate      Does a service using
                              labels on archived images?  the model find more
                                                          TB, and harm fewer
                                                          people?
  Population                  Whoever is in the dataset   Whoever walks in, at
                                                          local prevalence
  Reference standard          Dataset labels, of varying  Bacteriological
                              origin                      confirmation, where
                                                          reachable
  Failure visibility          Visible in the metrics      Often invisible until
                                                          someone is missed
  Threshold                   Tuned on held-out data      Must be recalibrated,
                                                          then monitored
  A good result licenses      A prospective study         Use in care, under
                                                          governance, with audit

10.3 Conclusion

  What this prototype demonstrates
    A complete interface and reporting structure for a chest X-ray screening
    model: the input contract, the output format, the evaluation framework,
    and the disclosures that have to travel with a result.

  What it does not demonstrate
    That a model can screen for TB from a chest radiograph. No model is
    connected, nothing has been trained, and no measurement has been taken.

  On RQ1, detection
    Unanswered. Answering it needs a patient-level split, a frozen test set,
    and sensitivity and specificity reported together with intervals.

  On RQ2, generalization
    Unanswered, and the hardest of the three. It needs images from a source
    that took no part in development.

  On RQ3, interpretability
    Unanswered. The review protocol is written; the saliency maps that belong
    in it have to come from real model weights.

  What would change the conclusion
    A trained model, a patient-level split, one external dataset, and a
    calibration curve. In that order.

  The sentence to leave with
    A chest radiograph can raise the suspicion of tuberculosis. It cannot
    confirm it, and neither can a model that reads one. Whatever a future
    version of this system reports, the useful output is a reason to test
    somebody, never a conclusion about them.
